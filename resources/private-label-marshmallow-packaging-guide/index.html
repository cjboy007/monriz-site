Private Label Marshmallow Packaging Guide

Packaging decides three things before price comes up: whether the product survives the voyage, whether the label clears customs, and whether your minimum order is a full container of one SKU or a mixed load you can sell.

This guide covers pack formats, the Arabic label, the moisture problem, and the numbers to confirm before you approve artwork.

The short version

Choose the pack format first, because it sets the carton, the freight and the MOQ math. Choose the label second, because it can block the shipment by itself. Approve artwork last, after the label has been checked against your market's rules.

For marshmallows, volume is the constraint. A 100 g bag holds little weight and a lot of air, so a container cubes out before it reaches its weight limit, and freight is quoted on cubic metres.

Step two: film and the moisture problem

Marshmallow is hygroscopic. It takes moisture from humid air, goes sticky, then loses shape. The pack holds the product's moisture in and the destination's humidity out.

StructureWhere it fitsTrade off
BOPP or CPPPillow bags, short sea routes, dry destinationsLowest cost, modest barrier
PET and PE laminateStand-up pouches and premium bagsBetter barrier and print quality, higher cost
Metallised or foil laminateLong routes, humid destinations, longer shelf life targetsHighest cost, foil creases show on shelf

Test three things before the run: seal strength, drop performance of the filled carton, and humidity after a few weeks in a container. A leaking seal means a returned container.

Step three: the Arabic label

Gulf markets require Arabic, alone or alongside another language. The Gulf standard for labelling prepackaged food is GSO 9, and Saudi Arabia cites the 2022 edition. The label is checked at the port by an inspector who does not care how the design looks.

FieldDetail that gets shipments held
Product nameA name buyers and customs recognise, not an internal code
Brand and manufacturerLegal manufacturer name plus the plant address
Country of originPrinted, not implied by a flag or a barcode
IngredientsDescending by weight, allergens declared
Net contentMetric, in the unit your market expects
Production and expiry datesPrinted, stamped or embossed by the producer. A sticker applied later is a common rejection reason
Storage conditions and lot codeTemperature and humidity statement, traceable batch code
Nutrition declarationWhere your market requires one, under the Gulf nutrition labelling standard
Importer detailsName and address in the destination market, where required

Five rules worth writing into the print spec:

  1. Use Western digits with Arabic text unless your importer asks otherwise, and keep it consistent across fields.
  2. Arabic runs right to left, but numbers and dates read left to right inside it. Proof with a native reader, not a translation tool.
  3. Keep text at least 3 mm clear of seals and fold lines.
  4. The GS1 prefix shows which GS1 member organisation issued the number, not where the goods were made.
  5. No halal word or logo without a certificate. In Saudi Arabia a product carrying either without a valid certificate can be refused.

Step four: the MOQ checklist

Get each line in writing in the quotation before artwork approval.

ItemWhy it decides your order

PalletisationPalletised loads lose 10 to 15 per cent of usable cube
Container typeA 20ft holds about 33 cubic metres, a 40ft high cube about 76. Pallet counts run 9 to 10 industrial pallets in a 20ft and 20 to 21 in a 40ft
Shelf life at shipmentGulf buyers often ask for minimum remaining shelf life on arrival. Fix the number in the contract

Container math, done plainly. Divide usable volume by carton volume, then take 10 per cent off for stowage. A 0.06 cubic metre carton gives about 500 cartons in a 20ft at nominal volume, closer to 450 after stowage. Multiply by units per carton and you have your true minimum order. Compare it with the quoted MOQ.

Step five: sampling, in the order it happens

  1. Brief. Product line, piece weight, colour, flavour, pack format and target market. One page is enough.
  2. Recipe and format matching. The supplier confirms which line and film can carry the brief.
  3. Label template. A die line with the safe print area marked.
  4. Pre-press proof. Artwork on the template, returned for sign off. The cheapest place to catch an Arabic error.
  5. Sample production. A small run on the actual line, not a hand made sample.
  6. Courier, feedback, and written approval of recipe, pack and artwork with dates.

Sample lead time and courier cost are confirmed per SKU. A new shape adds sampling time, so treat any date given before the mould exists as provisional.

Step six: five failures worth designing out

  1. Arabic that looks right in a PDF and breaks on a curved pouch. Check a printed sample.
  2. Colour drift between digital proof and printed film. Ask for a physical proof, or accept a tolerance in writing.
  3. Expiry date stamped over artwork, or missing on part of the run.
  4. Bottom layer cartons crushed because the stack exceeded the box compression strength.
  5. Barcode printed inside the seal or too close to the edge to scan.

FAQ

What is the minimum order for private label marshmallows?

One 20ft container, with mixed SKUs possible. The per SKU minimum is confirmed in the quotation, because it depends on the film and the print run.

Can you print our own artwork and brand?

Yes. Send artwork as AI or PDF. A pre-press proof comes back for approval, and label content is confirmed before production.

Do you support Arabic labels?

Yes, Arabic and English, including ingredients, production date and expiry date. Label requirements for your market stay with you as the buyer, and anything the proof cannot meet is flagged before print.

Who registers the barcode?

You do, with GS1 in your own country. Send the number with the artwork and we place it. A Chinese prefix does not make the product Chinese for origin purposes.
